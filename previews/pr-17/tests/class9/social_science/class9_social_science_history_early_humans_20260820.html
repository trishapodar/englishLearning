For over two million years, early humans lived as nomadic hunter-gatherers, continuously following animal herds and seasonal wild plants. Around 10,000 to 12,000 years ago, a profound transformation known as the Neolithic Revolution occurred. Humans learned to sow seeds, cultivate crops such as barley, wheat, and rice, and domesticate herd animals like sheep, goats, and cattle.

Farming anchored communities to one place, leading to permanent mud-brick villages, specialized tool-making, weaving, and ceramic pottery. As agricultural efficiency increased, farms yielded surplus food beyond immediate subsistence needs. This surplus freed a portion of the population from direct food production, fostering the rise of artisans, traders, administrators, and religious specialists, ultimately paving the way for the world's earliest cities.

Section B

Q5. Microliths and Climatic Warming
Model Answer & Marking: (a) Definition of Microliths: Microliths were tiny, sharp, symmetrical stone tools (usually 1 to 5 cm long) crafted during the Mesolithic period, frequently mounted on wooden or bone handles to make composite tools like spears, arrows, and sickles. [1 Mark]
(b) Impact of Climatic Warming: Warmer climates after the Ice Age led to the expansion of grasslands and flora, increasing the population of herbivores (deer, sheep, cattle) and enabling humans to shift from heavy big-game hunting to fishing, small-game hunting, and the initial taming/herding of animals. [1 Mark]
Q6. Burzahom Settlement
Model Answer & Marking: Features of Burzahom (Any two):
Pit-Houses: People dug circular and oval pit-dwellings into the ground with steps leading down, providing insulation against severe cold. [1 Mark]
Specialized Tools: Polished stone axes and a rich variety of bone tools (needles, harpoons, points) were discovered. [1 Mark]
Burial Customs: Evidence of unique burials, including humans buried alongside their domesticated animals (like dogs). (Alternative accepted).

Section C

Q7. Role of Fire
Model Answer & Marking: Three Transformative Roles of Fire:
Protection & Warmth: Fire provided essential warmth during cold nights/ice ages and kept wild predators away from cave entrances. [1 Mark]
Cooking & Nutrition: Cooking softened tough raw meat and roots, making food easier to digest, safer from pathogens, and improving overall health. [1 Mark]
Light & Extended Activity: Provided illumination inside dark caves, extending productive hours for tool-making and social interaction. [1 Mark]
Q8. Early Civilisations in River Valleys
Model Answer & Marking: Three Reasons for Emergence in River Valleys:
Fertile Alluvial Soil: Annual river floods deposited rich silt on the floodplains, ensuring high crop yields without exhaustive soil management. [1 Mark]
Perennial Water Supply: Rivers provided an abundant, reliable supply of water for human and animal consumption as well as irrigation for agricultural fields. [1 Mark]
Trade and Transportation: Waterways served as the earliest and most cost-effective transportation highways for trading heavy goods, metals, and connecting distant settlements. [1 Mark]

Section D

Q9. Case-Based Model Answers
Case-Based Model Answers & Marking:
Part (a) Shift in Food Procurement [1.5 Marks]: Palaeolithic Era: Humans were nomadic food gatherers and hunters, relying entirely on wild nature and moving constantly in search of animal herds and wild plants. [0.75 Mark]. Neolithic Era: Humans became settled food producers (farmers and herders), systematically cultivating crops (wheat, barley, rice) and domesticating cattle, sheep, and goats. [0.75 Mark]

Part (b) Need for Settled Life and Pottery [2 Marks]: Permanent Settlements: Crops required continuous care over several months—sowing, watering, weeding, and protecting plants until harvest—forcing humans to build permanent dwellings near their farmlands. [1 Mark]. Invention of Pottery: Surplus grain, seeds for the next sowing cycle, and milk/cooked liquids required durable, pest-proof, and fire-resistant storage containers, leading to the invention of earthenware and the potter's wheel. [1 Mark]

Part (c) Agricultural Surplus to Urban Civilisation [2.5 Marks]: Food Security & Population Growth: Efficient farming produced more food than farming families consumed, supporting larger and denser populations. [0.5 Mark]. Division of Labour & Craft Specialisation: Because not everyone needed to farm, individuals could specialize in full-time non-farming professions (e.g., metalworkers, potters, masons, weavers, toolmakers). [1 Mark]. Trade, Governance, and Urban Growth: Specialised craft production required raw materials (copper, tin, timber) from distant regions, driving long-distance trade, the invention of scripts/weights for record-keeping, centralized administrative systems, and the growth of trading hubs into the world's first cities. [1 Mark]
