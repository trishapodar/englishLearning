Q3. (D) 40 square units
Marking Scheme / Steps: Find the side length of the original quadrilateral ABCD using the distance formula: AB = √((-1 - 2)² + (2 - 1)²) = √((-3)² + 1²) = √(9 + 1) = √(10) units.
Verify that ABCD is a square by checking that all sides are equal (AB = BC = CD = DA = √(10) units) and the diagonals are equal (AC = BD = √(20) units).
Calculate the area of the square ABCD: Area = (side)² = (√(10))² = 10 square units.
Since the coordinates of the vertices of PQRS are doubled, the scale factor of the linear dimensions is k = 2.
The area of a 2D figure scaled by a factor of k increases by k². Therefore, the area of PQRS = 2² × Area(ABCD) = 4 × 10 = 40 square units. Alternatively, calculating the side length PQ = √((-2 - 4)² + (4 - 2)²) = √(40) units gives the area as (√(40))² = 40 square units.
Q4. (C) √((x_2 - x_1)² + (y_2 - y_1)²)
Marking Scheme / Steps: Recall that the distance between any two points P(x_1, y_1) and Q(x_2, y_2) is derived using the Baudhāyana–Pythagoras Theorem.
The horizontal distance between the points is given by |x_2 - x_1| and the vertical distance is given by |y_2 - y_1|.
Applying the theorem, the distance PQ is the square root of the sum of the squares of these differences: PQ = √((x_2 - x_1)² + (y_2 - y_1)²).

Tossing a coin is fair because it is a random experiment where the two possible outcomes (Heads and Tails) are equally likely, each having a probability of 0.5. Since the outcome of any single toss is completely unpredictable in advance, neither team has an unfair advantage.

The student's claim is mathematically incorrect because each coin toss is an independent event. The coin has no memory of past outcomes. Therefore, the probability of getting a tail on the sixth toss remains exactly 0.5, regardless of the previous five heads.

(a) The outcomes greater than 4 on a six-sided die are 5, 6 (2 favorable outcomes out of 6 total outcomes). Thus, the theoretical probability is: P(greater than 4) = 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.33
(b) For 600 rolls, the expected frequency is: Expected Frequency = 600 × 1/3 = 200 times. This relates to the long-run behavior because while we cannot predict the outcome of any single roll, probability theory guarantees that as the number of trials increases, the empirical frequency of an event will closely approach its theoretical probability.
