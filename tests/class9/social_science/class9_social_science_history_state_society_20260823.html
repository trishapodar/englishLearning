In ancient and early medieval India, political authority was not merely concentrated in the royal court; it relied on structured tiers of provincial governance and autonomous village communities. Under dynasties like the Pallavas, Chalukyas, and Cholas, administrative divisions stretched from the province (Mandalam) down to districts (Nadu) and village clusters (Kurram).

Village settlements, especially Brahmadeyas (tax-free lands gifted to learned Brahmins), developed sophisticated self-governing assemblies known as the Sabha. Inscriptions, such as those at Uttaramerur, reveal that these assemblies managed local taxation, desilted water reservoirs, maintained roads, and dispensed local justice.

Overarching all political power was the moral imperative of Dharma and Rajadharma. A king was not an absolute tyrant; his supreme duty was the protection of society, preservation of justice (Nyaya), and upholding righteous order.

Section B

Q5. Sabha vs. Samiti and Monarchy vs. Gaṇasaṅgha
Model Answer & Marking:
Part (a) Sabha vs. Samiti [1 Mark]:
Sabha: A smaller, advisory council consisting of village elders, prominent chiefs, and learned individuals that performed judicial and elite administrative functions. [0.5 Mark]
Samiti: A larger, general assembly representing the common people/tribe that gathered to discuss collective policy, state affairs, and confirm royal leadership. [0.5 Mark]
Part (b) Monarchy vs. Gaṇasaṅgha [1 Mark]:
Monarchy (Rajya): Centralized rule where political power is hereditary and vested in a single king (Rajan) advised by ministers. [0.5 Mark]
Gaṇasaṅgha (Republic/Oligarchy): Power is decentralized and vested in an assembly (Santhagara) of chosen clan representatives or chiefs (Rajas) who take decisions through collective debate and consensus/voting. [0.5 Mark]
Q6. Chakravarti Samrāṭ and Aśhvamedha Yajña
Model Answer & Marking:
Concept of Chakravarti Samrāṭ [1 Mark]: It represents the political ideal of a righteous, pan-Indian universal emperor whose sovereign authority and moral rule (Dharma) unite diverse regions of the subcontinent under a unified civilisational and political framework. [1 Mark]
Significance of the Aśhvamedha Yajña [1 Mark]: A royal horse sacrifice performed by powerful kings to demonstrate political sovereignty and territorial supremacy. Regions traversed by the royal horse unchallenged acknowledged the king’s overlordship, creating interstate alliances without constant bloodshed. [1 Mark]

Section C

Q7. Kautilya’s Saptāṅga Theory (Lumbs of State)
Model Answer & Marking: (Award 1 Mark for each valid limb correctly identified and explained — Any 3):
Amātya (Ministers and Bureaucracy): High-ranking officials, counsellors, and executive administrators who run daily state affairs, formulate policy, collect intelligence, and enforce law. [1 Mark]
Janapada (Territory and Population): The agricultural land, fertile countryside, mines, forests, and industrious tax-paying populace that form the economic backbone of the state. [1 Mark]
Durga (Fortified Capital / Forts): Strongly fortified strongholds on borders and capital cities to defend against foreign invasions and protect royal assets and citizens during war. [1 Mark]
Koṣha (Treasury): Accumulated legitimate wealth and bullion stored in state vaults to finance public works, famine relief, army salaries, and infrastructure development. [1 Mark]
Daṇḍa / Bala (Military / Armed Force): A well-trained standing army composed of infantry, cavalry, chariots, and elephants to maintain internal law and order and repel external aggressors. [1 Mark]
Mitra (Allies): Loyal and trusted foreign rulers and diplomatic allies whose support provides geopolitical security and balance of power. [1 Mark]
Q8. Rise of Magadha
Model Answer & Marking: (Award 1 Mark for each well-explained factor — Total 3 Marks):
Rich Mineral Resources (Iron Ore Deposits): Magadha had access to rich iron ore deposits in the nearby Rajgir/Gaya region (modern Jharkhand/Bihar), enabling the mass production of superior iron weapons and sturdy iron ploughshares for clearing dense Gangetic forests. [1 Mark]
Fertile Alluvial Soil & Agricultural Surplus: Situated along the fertile floodplains of the Ganga, Son, and Gandak rivers, Magadha enjoyed abundant rainfall and perennial water supply, generating substantial agrarian surpluses and steady tax revenue to maintain large standing armies. [1 Mark]
Strategic Geography & Military Advantages: Magadha's first capital, Rajagriha, was surrounded by five natural hill barriers, making it impregnable to siege. Its later capital, Pataliputra, was a water fortress (Jaladurga) situated at river confluences, facilitating rapid troop movement and riverine trade control. Magadha was the first power to extensively deploy wild war elephants captured from eastern forests to shatter enemy ranks and wooden fortifications. [1 Mark]

Section D

Q9. Case-Based Model Answers
Case-Based Model Answers & Rubric:
Part (a) Chola Administrative Hierarchy [1.5 Marks]: Administrative Hierarchy (Descending order): Mandalam: Province (often governed by royal princes or governors). [0.5 Mark]. Valanadu / Nadu: District / Group of villages overseen by local councils (Nattar). [0.5 Mark]. Kurram / Gramam: Village cluster or basic rural administrative unit. [0.5 Mark]

Part (b) Brahmadeya & Committee (Variyam) System [2 Marks]: Definition of Brahmadeya: A tax-exempt land grant given by kings to learned Brahmins or scholars, which evolved into centers of learning, advanced agriculture, and self-governance. [1 Mark]. The Variyam System: The Sabha delegated specialized municipal responsibilities to sub-committees called Variyams (e.g., Eri-variyam for tank and water management, Thotta-variyam for garden and land management, Pon-variyam for gold and finances). [0.5 Mark]. Members (Variyaperumakkal) were selected via a lottery system (Kudavolai) from qualified candidates who met strict age, educational, property, and moral integrity criteria, ensuring accountability. [0.5 Mark]

Part (c) Ethical Limits on Monarchy & Rajadharma [2.5 Marks]: Primacy of Dharma over Monarchy: In Indian political philosophy, the king was not above the law; he was the custodian and servant of Dharma, not its creator. His authority derived legitimacy only as long as he upheld righteous conduct. [1 Mark]. Core Principles of Rajadharma: Welfare of the People (Yogakshema): As stated in ancient texts and Arthashastra: "In the happiness of his subjects lies the king's happiness, in their welfare his welfare." [0.75 Mark]. Impartial Justice (Nyaya): The king was required to dispense equal and non-discriminatory justice using Danda (punishment) only strictly in accordance with customary law and moral justice, preventing tyranny. [0.75 Mark]
